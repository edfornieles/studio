# New York New York Happy Happy (NY NY HP HP)

## Short description

A semi-fictional charity benefit / art gala staged as immersive performance. Commissioned by Rhizome as a fundraiser, Fornieles mapped the narratives and character types of New York society life onto a real gala and its online representation, drawing on film/TV (Patrick Bateman, Woody Allen, Sex and the City, Girls). Ticket-buyers were contacted in advance and assigned heightened personas with cues; both real museum donors and hired actors were scripted, blurring reality and fiction.

## Core question

What is the difference between a real social ritual and its performed/fictional double when participants and their online traces are scripted?

## Social machine

A charity-gala format as a scripted social system — pre-assigned personas and cues for real donors + actors, plus its social-media documentation.

## Fictional device

The "semi-fictional benefit": a real Rhizome fundraising gala (real ticket tiers, $50 "bronze" to $5,000 "black," advertised perks like "dedicated arm candy" and limo rides) doubled as a scripted fiction. Real Museum donors and hired actors were given personas in advance; actors with "hidden agendas" were seeded among genuine guests so that no participant could reliably tell who was performing and who was sincere. (Fact: Wikipedia, W Magazine, Vice.)

## Real-world system it attaches to

Art-world charity galas / benefit fundraisers; New York downtown society and its archetypes; celebrity and pop-culture image; social media documentation. Inspiration mapped from film and TV — Patrick Bateman, Woody Allen, *Sex and the City*, *Girls*. (Fact: Wikipedia.)

## Audience / participant role

Ticket-buyers became scripted players. Fornieles contacted attendees in advance (Vice's writer got a phone call the morning of the event) and assigned a "heightened persona" plus cues to perform across the night. Documented roles: "Sociopath / a Patrick Bateman type," "Selfie," "E-Cig," "Art Talk," "Gossip Queen" — downtown art-world caricatures. The audience was simultaneously the fundraiser's donor base and the cast of the fiction. (Fact: Interview, Vice, Wikipedia.)

## Performer / subject role

Two blurred tiers: (1) hired actors/dancers carrying undisclosed agendas, and (2) real donors performing assigned characters. Named participant-performers in press: Hari Nef (lip-synch performance), Genevieve Belleveau, Labanna Babylon, Contessa Stuto. Chippendales-style male strippers escalated the room. Fornieles himself appeared in a white suit slashed with "cascading purple Yves Klein paint." (Fact: Interview, W Magazine.)

## Emotional engine

Suspicion, complicity and loss of footing: not knowing who is acting, being drawn into cued intimacy (holding hands, then being undressed by dancers), and the giddy/uneasy slide from decorous gala into hedonistic chaos. Drives the thrill of a charity event into something closer to a controlled social experiment. (Inference from Vice/W Magazine reportage.)

## Ethical danger

Consent and disclosure. Scripting *real* Museum donors and seeding undisclosed actors made the work controversial — guests had bras removed, were bitten, ate salami off near-naked performers, and the New Museum director reportedly reacted with alarm ("did you see that man take his pants off in front of me?"). The line between immersive art and non-consensual manipulation of paying patrons is the live danger. (Fact for controversy: Wikipedia, W Magazine; inference on consent.)

## Aesthetic world

Tags: gala/benefit, society archetypes, immersive performance, role-play, social ritual, online representation, Rhizome/Performa.

## Research method

Casting and pre-scripting: identifying the archetypes that structure NY downtown/society life from film and TV, then assigning them as personas to real attendees and actors, with advance contact (phone calls/briefings) and cues. The real charity-gala apparatus (ticket tiers, donors, venue) supplies the host system the fiction parasitizes. (Fact: Wikipedia, Vice.)

## Use of real material

A genuine Rhizome benefit at a real institution (New Museum / Performa 13), with real donors, real money/ticket tiers, the real New Museum director present, and real social-media documentation. The fundraiser was not simulated — it actually raised funds while being performed. (Fact: Wikipedia, Interview, W Magazine.)

## Use of fiction

Personas, cues, plot agendas and undisclosed actors overlaid on the real event so that authenticity itself becomes undecidable. The fiction is not staged on a stage; it is distributed invisibly through the crowd. (Fact: W Magazine, Wikipedia.)

## Emergent behaviour

The scripted gala "quickly unraveled": cued intimacy escalated, dancers stripped guests, a "human meat pile" formed, a pillow fight tipped into the room being "completely decimated around 11PM." Emergence ran past the script into genuine collective chaos. (Fact: Vice, W Magazine, Interview.)

## Residue / documentation / afterlife

Press coverage (Interview, W Magazine, Vice, Rhizome editorial), photographic documentation, and the work's entry into Fornieles' gallery record (courtesy Carlos/Ishikawa; catalogued as 2014). Images catalogued in image_manifests/public_gallery_manifest.jsonl (project: ny-ny-hp-hp); incoming manifest at image_manifests/_incoming/ny-ny-hp-hp.jsonl. (Fact.)

## What this project teaches the studio

That a real social institution can be run as a playable system: hand participants archetypes and cues and the institution's own rituals will do the dramaturgy. Also a caution — the most charged material (undisclosed actors among paying donors) is exactly where consent and institutional trust are most at risk. (Inference grounded in sources.)

## What failed or remained unresolved

The control problem: the event's slide into "decimation" and the recorded alarm of the institution suggest the work outran its own frame, leaving open whether it was critique or simply manipulation of guests who had not fully consented to being characters. (Inference from W Magazine/Vice reportage.)

## Possible mutations

Versions with explicit consent/disclosure tiers; smaller-scale "scripted dinner" formats; an online-only persona-assignment layer; reuse of the archetype-casting method on other ritual institutions (weddings, conferences, award shows — cf. The Dreamy Awards). (Inference.)
