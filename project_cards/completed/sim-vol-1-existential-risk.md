# Sim Vol. 1: Existential Risk

## Short description

A simulation / role-play work realised as a two-channel installation. Four
players (Harrie, Sasha, Jesse, Rebecca) in Middelburg, Netherlands, played out an
epidemic scenario over a Google Hangout with a Montréal-based Project Manager
(Fornieles); their narrated testimonies drive one projection while a second shows
3D animations made with forensic-imaging techniques. A display case holds the
"Game Book," the manual to the simulation. Scenarios escalate from sick players
cared for at risk of infection to a player clubbing a child character to death
because it threatens the group's security. (Fact: SCHIRN PEACE, aqnb.)

## Core question

How do groups rationalize cruelty, risk and sacrifice under the rule-systems of a
simulation — and what does that reveal about real social behaviour, security, and
the limits of self-determination? The work shifts from the danger of dying in an
epidemic into love and affection, "emerging as a discourse on peace and freedom
of will and action." (Fact: SCHIRN framing; question is inference.)

## Social machine

A Sims-inspired live role-play with imposed rule-sets, run by a Project Manager
who proposes scenarios while players act through avatars guided by their own
"limitations, strengths and moral code." A deep profile-creation process places
"emphasis on the players being themselves," so the simulation runs on real
personalities, not invented characters. A "Game Book" codifies the rules. (Fact:
aqnb / SCHIRN.)

## Fictional device

The "Game Book" — a fictional rulebook/manual whose section headings ("Emotional
Confrontation," "A Last Walk," "No Escape," "Death as Gateway") and the
illustration "Multidimensional Being" frame death and survival as game mechanics.
The epidemic/apocalypse scenario (atomic clouds, alien film posters) is the
fictional shell around real testimony. (Fact: headings/illustration confirmed.)

## Real-world system it attaches to

Video-game simulation (The Sims as reference point); existential-risk and
systems modelling; epidemiology / quarantine ethics; forensic 3D imaging.
(Fact: The Sims reference and forensic imaging from coverage; existential-risk
framing is the work's own title.)

## Audience / participant role

Gallery viewers receive the work as documentary-style testimony plus animation —
witnesses to a game already played. The *active* participants were the four
remote role-players who lived the scenario. (Fact: two-channel testimony format;
players were remote, not gallery visitors.)

## Performer / subject role

The four players are simultaneously performer, subject and author of their own
moral choices — "being themselves" rather than acting a part, so the emotional
stakes are real even though the events are simulated. Fornieles plays Project
Manager, proposing situations rather than dictating outcomes. (Fact.)

## Emotional engine

Real care, fear and guilt loaded into fictional acts: players nurse the sick at
genuine felt risk; the killing of a child "for security" is narrated with real
moral weight. Fornieles "integrates personal relationships and emotional
dimensions into simulated actions" rather than maintaining fictional boundaries.
(Fact: SCHIRN.)

## Ethical danger

Eliciting genuine cruelty, sacrifice and trauma from real people under a game
frame — most starkly, a player killing a child character because it "puts the
security of the community at risk." The work tests how readily ordinary people
rationalize violence as collective safety. (Fact: scenario confirmed; the danger
reading is inference.)

## Aesthetic world

Tags: simulation, The Sims, role-play, existential risk, emergent behaviour, game
logic, rulebook, epidemic, apocalypse, forensic imaging. Opens with upbeat music
over apocalyptic imagery (atomic clouds, alien-themed film posters); dual
projection of talking-head testimony + forensic 3D animation; vitrine with the
Game Book.

## Research method

Systems-building plus participatory role-play: build players' real-self profiles,
impose a rule-set and scenarios via a Project Manager, run it over video calls,
then harvest the testimony and re-render events as forensic animation. "I set up
a bunch of parameters; then people, images or algorithms play themselves out."
(Fact: process from aqnb; quote from Le Random.)

## Use of real material

Real participants playing themselves; their actual moral codes, relationships and
emotional reactions; documentary testimony to camera; forensic-imaging technique
borrowed from crime reconstruction. Fornieles: "I am a thief, not a maker."
(Fact: profile process, forensic imaging, quote.)

## Use of fiction

The epidemic/existential-risk scenario, the Game Book and its death-themed
chapter headings, and the avatar frame — a thin fictional membrane that lets real
people commit to extreme acts. (Fact: Game Book; framing is inference.)

## Emergent behaviour

The dark social behaviour is not scripted — it emerges from players, rules and
scenario interacting: spontaneous care under infection risk, and the
rationalized killing of a child for group security. This is Fornieles' "at its
best it's terrifyingly emergent. Roleplay is a good example of this." (Fact:
behaviours; quote Le Random.)

## Residue / documentation / afterlife

Installation views and stills on OFLUXO (catalogued in
image_manifests/_incoming/sim-vol-1-existential-risk.jsonl); reviewed by aqnb;
re-presented as a two-channel installation in SCHIRN's PEACE programme (Frankfurt,
2017, text by Kristin Schrader). A Schirn video interview also exists. Numbered
"Vol. 1," implying an intended series. (Fact.)

## What this project teaches the studio

That making participants "be themselves" inside a rule-set produces real ethical
stakes and genuinely emergent behaviour — the studio's strongest engine for
truth is a well-built game played by real people. Also: a manual/Game Book can do
heavy conceptual and aesthetic work as an object. (Inference.)

## What failed or remained unresolved

The duty-of-care question: extracting real cruelty and grief from real players
raises consent/aftercare issues the public sources do not resolve. "Vol. 1"
implies sequels; no confirmed Vol. 2 located. How much the Project Manager steered
vs. let emerge is unclear. (Inference / research gap.)

## Possible mutations

Later volumes with new scenarios; larger player pools; live-audience
participation; coupling its narrated moral stakes to [[truth-table]]'s randomized
bodies; publishing the Game Book as a standalone artist's manual. (Inference.)

## Assistant behaviour rules derived from this project

When Ed proposes a role-play/simulation, ask: who plays, are they "being
themselves," what is the rule-set, who is Project Manager, and what is the
aftercare. Treat the rulebook/Game Book as a primary artwork, not admin. Name the
consent/ethics surface early when real people enact extreme acts. (Inference.)
