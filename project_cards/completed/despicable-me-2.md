# Despicable Me 2 (incl. 'Pony Hoof')

## Short description

An exhibition organised around a fabricated virtual muse ('Britney Rivers'), using candy colours, glitter, mirrors and fairy lights alongside darker, self-mocking transgression. The sculpture 'Pony Hoof' — a large soft toy brutally impaled on a gallows — pairs infantile aesthetics with violence.

## Core question

How do childhood/infantile aesthetics conceal or coexist with violence and cruelty?

## Social machine

A gallery installation organised around a fabricated social-media persona (Britney Rivers).

## Fictional device

A fabricated muse, "Britney Rivers" (@britriversxoxo; facebook.com/britney.rivers.33) — "the epitome of Generation Y narcissism and vapidity," who lives on Facebook and Instagram. Fornieles positions her as "someway between a virtual muse and a subjective lens": he wants the viewer to believe she is "calling the aesthetic shots, even if she only truly exists in his imagination" (Griffin, ArtReview). The press release adopts a "reality TV format" following Rivers "as she navigates her way online: making friends, gaining likes and becoming the star of her own online reality" (Nicodim).

## Real-world system it attaches to

Social-media self-presentation and metrics (likes, selfies, friend networks); Generation Y / millennial network culture; consumer kids' and cartoon culture (Family Guy's Stewie Griffin, soft toys, candy, glitter).

## Audience / participant role

Gallery viewer and, by extension, social-media follower — Griffin notes "I'm Facebook friends with her. You should be too," so the audience can become part of Rivers's network beyond the room. In the gallery the viewer navigates a deliberately illegible "sprawl of objects placed on rectangles of carpet."

## Performer / subject role

The fictional persona Rivers is the nominal author/subject; Fornieles is the hidden operator who "holds the passwords to her online profiles." Real internet-sourced people appear as raw material: back-of-space portraits are "headshots of three young women and one man, culled from the Internet, with teeth or eyebrows digitally erased" (Griffin).

## Emotional engine

Seduction-and-revulsion: candy colours, glitter, mirrors and fairy lights pull the viewer in while violence and abjection (an impaled soft toy, an erect-penis lightbox, chewed bubblegum) push back. The works' "obvious relation to infantilism is conflicted and self-mocking." The mood is ambivalent rather than critical.

## Ethical danger

Scraping and altering real people's images (portraits "culled from the Internet" with features digitally erased); operating a persona who passes as a real woman online while the artist controls her accounts; pairing infantile/childhood aesthetics with violence and explicit sexual imagery. Griffin asks directly: "Is it she or he who is despicable? Probably both."

## Aesthetic world

Tags: infantilism, cuteness, violence, sculpture, soft toy, fiction, fabricated persona.

## Research method

Build a persona, run her across live social platforms (Facebook/Instagram/Twitter), then let her supposed taste dictate the physical exhibition — a "reality TV format" (Nicodim). The installation "functions like a set wherein objecthood and value-systems are in flux," with objects acting as "performative nodes" linking to wider reference networks. Internet-harvested imagery is collected and digitally altered as source material.

## Use of real material

Real internet photographs of real people (the four altered portraits "culled from the Internet"); live, functioning social-media accounts that real users could follow; pop-cultural artefacts (Stewie Griffin casts, soft toys, bubblegum, candy). Rivers herself is embodied by "a flesh-and-blood, twenty-something woman" in Instagram selfies (Griffin).

## Use of fiction

The entire show is attributed to a fictional author (Rivers), whose invented Gen-Y sensibility becomes the curatorial logic. "There are around 40 artworks on the checklist, but it is hard to discern which piece belongs to which title. This is surely deliberate" — the fiction of network culture is enacted as an illegible, hyperlinked sprawl (Griffin).

## Emergent behaviour

The persona persists and accrues a real social-media presence beyond the gallery (live accounts, real followers), so Rivers keeps "performing" after the show — an afterlife the artist only partly controls. (Inference, grounded in the live accounts noted by Griffin.)

## What this project teaches the studio

- A fictional author can become the curatorial intelligence of a show — let an invented persona "choose" the aesthetic.
- Infantile/cute aesthetics are a vehicle for, not the opposite of, transgression and violence.
- Ambivalence can be the point: the work need not resolve into critique ("less an ideological critique than an ambivalent embodiment").
- A persona that lives on real platforms has a life and ethics beyond the gallery.

## What failed or remained unresolved

Critics read the show as complicit/ambivalent rather than critical, leaving open whether the satire lands or simply reproduces what it depicts ("Is it she or he who is despicable? Probably both"). The ethics of scraping and altering real strangers' faces is unresolved. To confirm with Ed: how much of Rivers was performed by a real collaborator vs. authored by him, and how Pony Hoof related to the Rivers fiction. (`needs_review`)

## Possible mutations

- Hand the persona to an AI that generates her posts and "curates" a future show autonomously.
- A version where the followers/network are made visible as the artwork.
- Strip the cute layer entirely and show only the violence, testing whether the seduction was load-bearing.

## Assistant behaviour rules derived from this project

- When an idea uses real strangers' images, flag scraping/alteration ethics immediately (cf. [[dorm-daze]]).
- Offer "let a fictional persona curate/author it" as a structuring device.
- Watch for cute-plus-violence pairings; name the ambivalence rather than forcing a moral.
