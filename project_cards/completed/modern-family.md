# Modern Family

## Short description

An exhibition at Chisenhale Gallery (2014) that shifted focus from American
college archetypes to family structures. It aggregated thousands of online
images and videos — organised by popularity and filtered through family roles —
into screen-based compositions, alongside self-fiction / fan-fiction writing.

## Core question

How do family roles provide people with narratives and identities? People are
born into family systems and assigned roles so early that they rarely question
their validity or imagine they could be different.

## Social machine

The family as a role system: oldest son, oldest daughter, youngest daughter,
mother, father, teenage boy, teenage girl, child. Identity forms in opposition
to other family members and through inherited family language/protocol.

## Fictional device

Self-fiction and fan fiction: participants wrote short stories about themselves
inside an Americanised family experience, rehearsing family roles differently.

## Real-world system it attaches to

Online image/video culture and search popularity; the mutation of "family" in
popular imagination (1900 vs 1980s vs 2014 vs now), with the TV show *Modern
Family* as a cultural marker for new family forms (e.g. gay mothers/fathers).

## Audience / participant role

Gallery audience viewing aggregated screen compositions; participants in the
role-play/writing activity authoring family self-fiction.

## Performer / subject role

The "many" online subjects whose images are aggregated into composite role-types;
participants performing family roles through writing.

## Emotional engine

The uncanny familiarity of inherited family roles; recognition of how early and
how invisibly identity is assigned.

## Ethical danger

Aggregation of thousands of real online images and videos; representing real
people as raw material distilled by popularity into composite types.

## Aesthetic world

Screen-based installation: visual compositions of aggregated online imagery
displayed on screens. Influences: Robert Ashley (especially *Perfect Lives* /
the *Lives* opera) and Mike Kelley.

## Research method

Large-scale aggregation of online content; organising compositions by popularity
(popular searches filtered through family identities); facilitating self-fiction
writing.

## Use of real material

Thousands of real online images and videos, gathered and organised by popularity
through identity categories (mother, father, teenage boy, teenage girl, child).

## Use of fiction

The TV-referencing title; the self/fan fiction in which participants placed
themselves inside an Americanised family.

## Emergent behaviour

The aggregation distils the many into the one — composite role-types emerge from
the popularity logic rather than from authored choice.

## Residue / documentation / afterlife

Screen compositions and the body of participant-authored family self-fiction,
plus the immersive installation itself: an artificial LA-suburban backyard with
fake grass, lattices fitted with TVs, a polystyrene fireplace, a bed on a wooden
platform, plaster figures (e.g. a woman swinging a child), rusted/dirt-surfaced
metal panels, a hot tub disrupted by a wire sculpture, and wire texts reading
"Be Yourself" and "We are one." Documented in Andy Keate's installation views
for Chisenhale. The live online commission (the aggregating, Facebook-gated
website) was, by nature, ephemeral. TKTK — confirm which physical objects/archive
were retained after the show. (`needs_review`)

## What this project teaches the studio

- Family is a role system.
- Identity begins inside inherited protocol.
- Online aggregation can distil the many into one.
- Popularity can be used as an organising logic.
- Cultural categories mutate over time.
- Fan fiction / self-fiction lets people rehearse family roles differently.

## What failed or remained unresolved

The Facebook-login gate made participation a literal "trade of your photos or
data" — your images were then projected into the gallery for all to see. Public
reviews don't record uptake/refusal rates, so the real friction of that trade is
undocumented. Open questions to confirm with Ed: how legible the
popularity/aggregation logic was to audiences amid a deliberately chaotic,
decaying installation, and the precise relationship between the screen works and
the self-fiction writing activity. (`needs_review`)

## Possible mutations

- Re-run the aggregation on AI-generated families (model bias as the new
  "popularity").
- Invert the operation: one family exploded into every demographic the algorithm
  expects.
- A live family role-play cast by an AI from participants' data.

## Assistant behaviour rules derived from this project

- Recognise "aggregate the many into the one" as a core studio operation.
- Treat popularity / algorithmic ranking as an available organising logic.
- Surface how cultural categories mutate over time when dating an idea.
- Pair an aggregation work with a participatory self-fiction component.

## Research dossier (web, 2026-06-10)

*Modern Family* was Fornieles's **first major UK institutional solo**, at
**Chisenhale Gallery, London, 19 September – 9 November 2014** (preview 18 Sept).
The commission combined a **new online work** with sculpture and choreographed
performance inside an immersive installation that reimagined "the material logic
of home improvement, sitcom architecture and the shopping mall" as a distorted
"Pinterest reality." Materials mixed high-spec finishes and new technology with
breakfast cereal and DIY home tiling. The environment read as an LA-suburban
backyard in decay/excess — uneaten food piling up, muddied children's toys, a
fruit pie stuck to a trellis, an oversized banana, film-set lighting that cycled
and flickered, music alternating loud/quiet (Studio International; Chisenhale;
Meer press release).

**The online component:** a continually updated **aggregating website linked to
the APIs of Twitter, Tumblr, Instagram and Google** (Pinterest/Google Images
also cited), activated by search terms/hashtags, pulling thousands of live images
as a shifting backdrop projected across gallery screens. **Access required a
Facebook login** — Fornieles: "If you want access to the site, it's a trade of
your photos or data"; "I'm interested in… embodying a platform whereby I'm
trading in the commodity that is data." Visitors' own images were then projected
into the gallery for all to see. The work "enacts a collapse between the virtual
and the real, using the internet as raw material and recapitulating the collected
data back into sculpture, installation and performance," critiquing the attention
economy "where value is determined by likes, shares and reposts."

**Title/TV link:** the sitcom *Modern Family* is cited as marking "a genuine
mutation of what family is" — same-sex partnerships, blended households — "a
liberal version of the American family." Fornieles on lost passive media: "We
lost something when we lost TV, which was the ability to not click." Wire texts
"Be Yourself" / "We are one" stage the individual-vs-collective tension.
