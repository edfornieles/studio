# Animal House

## Short description

An early work using the American college party as a performative system.
Participants were each assigned four or five actions to perform through the
night; the party started small and escalated into a bacchanalian cacophony.

## Core question

What are the protocols and conventions that dictate behaviour — and what happens
when we playfully change the permissions available to participants?

## Social machine

The party as a social machine. A single-night live event with assigned roles and
actions, structured to escalate.

## Fictional device

Assigned characters and a script of 4–5 performable actions per guest, drawn from
online blogs, news stories, film and TV, books, personal experience, American
college rituals and media scenes.

## Real-world system it attaches to

The bacchanalian mode of the American college experience: hedonism, sexuality,
group identity, extreme rituals; fraternity/sorority hazing as a key reference.

## Audience / participant role

Every attendee is a performer. Each receives a small set of actions they can
perform through the night, occupying archetypal forms.

## Performer / subject role

Participants are puppeted by an archetype or cultural script — a libidinal,
sexual, hedonistic energy overtakes them.

## Emotional engine

Permission and release: assigned actions grant permission to behave otherwise;
excess and ritual produce a different experience of the self.

## Ethical danger

Hedonism, sexuality and extreme ritual (hazing) as material; escalation toward
excess and collapse in a live setting with real bodies.

## Aesthetic world

The American college party: red cups, glitter, alcohol-soaked materials, dirty
carpets, the debris of a night. A reality-TV / college-film / Hangover-film
register of escalation.

## Research method

Compiling actions from media and lived sources; designing an escalation
structure; facilitating a live emergent event.

## Use of real material

Real online blogs, news stories, personal experience and documented college
rituals fed the action scripts; the physical aftermath became real material.

## Use of fiction

Archetypal roles and assigned actions as a fictional overlay on a real party.

## Emergent behaviour

The party began with small actions and escalated into a bacchanalian cacophony,
following a reality-TV / Hangover logic where each scene must outdo the last.

## Residue / documentation / afterlife

The aftermath became material for later work: red cups, dirty carpets, glitter,
alcohol-soaked materials, installation remnants and documentary traces became
sculptural or archival material. The exhibition **The Hangover Part II** used
residue from Animal House and Dorm Daze — carpets, visual references,
photographs, information-aggregating screens, and sculptures dedicated to
characters.

## What this project teaches the studio

- The party can be a social machine.
- Ritual and excess can produce identity.
- Participants can be puppeted by an archetype or cultural script.
- Assigned actions produce permission.
- Escalation is a structural force.
- The residue of performance can become sculpture.
- The social event generates later objects, images and archives.

## What failed or remained unresolved

Public press records a "slightly slow start" before the crowd "fratted really
hard" — escalation needed active priming (Fornieles: "needs a bit of foreplay";
the first staged performance, a person cling-filmed/gaffa-taped to the bed,
licensed the rest). Open questions to confirm with Ed: the limits of escalation,
consent and safety in a live hedonistic event (guests were stripped, groped,
foamed), and how much of the night was steerable via the assigned actions vs.
genuinely emergent. (`needs_review`)

## Possible mutations

- An AI dungeon-master assigning and escalating actions in real time.
- A version where the residue is generated/curated as the work, party unseen.
- A post-AI Animal House (see eval prompts) where the script is model-authored.

## Assistant behaviour rules derived from this project

- Identify the assigned-action mechanism: what is each participant permitted to do?
- Treat escalation as a designable force, and watch its ethical ceiling.
- Always ask what residue the live event will leave and how it becomes an object.

## Research dossier (web, 2026-06-10)

Public press fixes the event: on a Saturday in mid-January 2011, Fornieles
(credited as Edward Fornieles) invited 120 people to **Guest Projects, London
Fields** for a staged American college party titled *Animal House* (Dazed,
17 Jan 2011). Participants RSVP'd via a Facebook event and were issued
**character descriptions** plus **actions to perform through the evening**,
sourced from college blogs, American students' Facebook profiles and films.

Scale and mechanics: filmed by **five camera crews**; **twelve actors and ~one
hundred invited extras** performed scenes "on loop" amid sculptural sets — a bed,
a white clapboard house and porch with a red front door, and a beer-filled fish
tank. Guests were gaffa-taped to walls, covered in shaving foam, stripped and
groped, and "encouraged generally to just 'let go.'" Escalation was primed: a
"slightly slow start" gave way once the first performance (a person
cling-filmed to the bed) modelled permission.

Research method: Fornieles watched frat-house films intensively — *Animal House*,
*Sorority Wars*, *Sorority Boys*, *Sorority Row*, *Road Trip Beer Pong*,
*American High Five* — and trawled college-prank blogs. He framed the work
through "cycles in people's lives" and "celebrations" that "expose this freedom
away from parents," and noted "the Atlantic ocean… simplifies America."

Lineage: *Animal House* sits in a series with the online **Dorm Daze** (2011), a
three-month semi-scripted Facebook "sitcom" of ~35 characters whose profiles
were "scalped from real life American college students" (Rhizome, Joanne McNeil,
2 Apr 2012; Hyperallergic, AX Mina, 5 Apr 2012). A related work, **Animal House
II: Revenge of the Nerdz** (2011), showed at the Royal College of Art. The
party's detritus fed Fornieles's debut solo, **The Hangover Part II**
(Carlos/Ishikawa, London, 11 Nov – 17 Dec 2011). Fornieles b. 1983, Petersfield;
Ruskin School, Oxford (BA 2005); RCA Sculpture MA (2011).
