# Dorm Daze

## Short description

An early work built around American college archetypes and Facebook-based
role-play. Participants occupied college characters online and developed them
over an extended period, using real accounts and scraped/observed material as a
starting point for fiction.

## Core question

How do narratives define who we are? How do inherited stories help us navigate
the world — telling us who we are, who others are, and how to interact?

## Social machine

Facebook-based role-play community organised around an American college world.
A persistent online social environment where participants performed characters
over time.

## Fictional device

Assigned/adopted college archetypes (jock, geek, sorority girl, frat guy, college
outsider) layered on top of real online identities, accounts and behaviours.

## Real-world system it attaches to

Facebook and online social platforms; the legible mythology of American college
culture as transmitted to England via film and popular culture; real news events
(e.g. the Occupy movement) metabolised into character narratives.

## Audience / participant role

Active role-players. Participants occupied shallow archetypes as vehicles to
explore parts of themselves, building character over a longer duration than
Animal House.

## Performer / subject role

The participants are the performers; their real online selves are the raw
material. Characters were seeded from real photos, likes, dislikes, images and
social codes.

## Emotional engine

The permission that fiction grants — the safety of a character as a place to try
on identity, desire and politics before living them openly.

## Ethical danger

Use of real accounts and scraped/observed material as fictional starting points;
the bleed between real lives and fictional narratives; intense escalation
(drugs, murder, exorcism, protest) inside a social space with real people.

## Aesthetic world

American college culture as known through films and popular culture: dorms,
sport, parties, sororities/fraternities — shallow but widely legible archetypes.
Residue images, posts and videos in a vernacular social-media aesthetic.

## Research method

Observation and use of real online material; building fiction on top of scraped
or observed reality; long-duration facilitation of an emergent social system.

## Use of real material

Real accounts, photos, likes/dislikes, behaviours, social codes — and real news
(Occupy) folded into characters' fictional lives.

## Use of fiction

Archetypal college characters as vehicles; narratives that escalated from dorm
life into epic drama. Fiction as a permission structure for real transformation.

## Emergent behaviour

Over time participants pushed through and broke their archetypes — duration gave
shallow types depth. Key example: Rob Chan, an English man in his 30s, played a
geeky character and through it explored his sexuality (gay identity and desire)
before doing so openly in real life. Narratives escalated with a TV/sequel logic
of intensification: from pizza parties and dorm life to drug gangs, murders,
exorcisms, and eventually protest/terrorist action against a bank.

## Residue / documentation / afterlife

Conversations, images, videos, posts and evidence of performance. These residues
later became material for installations and sculptures (see The Hangover Part II).

## What this project teaches the studio

- People perform inherited narratives.
- Archetypes can become vehicles for self-experimentation.
- Role-play changes the protocols of behaviour.
- Fiction can open real permission.
- Real news can be metabolised into personal fiction.
- Emergent systems generate content the artist could not have planned.
- Duration allows shallow roles to gain depth.

## What failed or remained unresolved

To confirm with Ed: where the escalation logic strained the system, the
ethics of real-material sourcing, and what was lost when narratives outdid
themselves. (`needs_review`)

Web sources (2026-06-10) document the escalation but not its breaking point:
Fornieles describes the network beginning in everyday sitcom realism (imaginary
parties bonding characters into the frat house, sorority, indie kids and
outsiders) before it "started to hyper-accelerate over a period of three months"
into "fantasy, horror and romance" — the system became "a very spongy place"
absorbing real current events (Occupy, Proposition 8) (Dazed, 2015; CURA.). The
ethics question is sharpened by the public language used about method: profiles
were "scalped" / "scraped" from real UC Berkeley students' public data
(Dazed; xenogothic, 2019). Public sources do not record where this strained
participants or what was lost at the peak of escalation — still open for Ed.

## Possible mutations

- Re-run on a contemporary platform with AI-driven NPCs alongside human players.
- A version where an AI watches and escalates the narrative automatically.
- Duration as the explicit subject: a years-long character pushed to breaking.

## Assistant behaviour rules derived from this project

- Treat duration as a tool: shallow roles gain depth only with time.
- When an idea uses real people's material, foreground the ethical danger early.
- Look for the permission structure a fiction grants a real participant.
- Expect and design for emergent escalation rather than fixed outcomes.

FACTS & QUOTES (from sources):
- Setting: a "Facebook Sitcom" set at the University of California, Berkeley; performers were
  London-based; run over three months in late 2011. (Dazed 2015; i-D)
- Participant count: given as 34 (Dazed 2015, i-D, CURA.) and as 35 characters (Hyperallergic 2012).
  The card says "35 actors" elsewhere in the dataset; sources disagree — confirm with Ed.
- Method language: profiles were "scalped" / "scraped" from real students' public Facebook data.
  (Dazed; xenogothic). xenogothic calls it a "Facebook LARP" producing "a twisted coming-of-age drama."
- Escalation: "Everything in the Dorm Daze network started to hyper-accelerate over a period of
  three months" — from sitcom realism into "fantasy, horror and romance"; became "a very spongy
  place" absorbing the Occupy movement and Proposition 8. (Dazed 2015 / CURA.)
- Archetype groups named in sources: frat house, sorority, indie kids, outsiders (also jocks, goths,
  sorority presidents). A Gossip Girl-style narrator commented on highlights. (Hyperallergic; Dazed)
- Fornieles quotes: "Characters informed their navigation of that with your previous experience of
  the real world; of its codes, conventions and understandings … you'd be neither acting nor gaming;
  it's more of a transferal of skills" (Hyperallergic). "Whatever is popular is powerful"; each work
  creates "a pocket of space to detach and observe themselves … this temporary point of detachment
  is super interesting" (i-D). "Dorm Daze aimed to unleash latent productive forces towards the
  building of this new fiction" (Dazed).
- Afterlife: writer Dean Kissick turned the raw material into a Tumblr novel (Dazed 2015); residue
  fed sculptures/installations including The Hangover Part II (2011). Documentation images on Dazed
  catalogued in image_manifests/_incoming/dorm-daze.jsonl.

DISCREPANCY WITH CARD PROSE (do not overwrite; flag for Ed): the card's emergent-behaviour example
names "Rob Chan, an English man in his 30s." Public sources instead name the player as Jon Ow,
playing a character called "Robby Chan," who used the role to explore romantic/sexual narratives and
to protest homophobia from the fictional frat house, folding in same-sex marriage / gay rights
(Dazed; corroborated reading). The card's account (Ed's own) is authoritative; the name/identity
detail differs from press and should be reconciled with Ed.
