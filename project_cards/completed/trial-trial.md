# Trial Trial

## Short description

A live, role-played court hearing (LARP) devised collectively by the n-Space fellows (dmstfctn, Ed Fornieles, Agnes Cameron, Hannah Cobb, Leela Jadhav) at Somerset House. It revives the medieval English 'deodand' rule — under which an object that caused a person's death could be seized and forfeited, holding its owner financially accountable — and imagines that law still operating today. A contemporary dual-use technology (one used in both civilian and military contexts) is put on trial as if it were legally responsible for harm, using the UK's reliance on governance-by-procurement as its frame. (Fact, per Somerset House.)

## Core question

Could existing British legal frameworks be used to hold complex technological systems morally and legally accountable — and how does a configuration of governance (here, military technology procured "as common-sense pragmatism through contracts and tenders") come to seem unavoidable and beyond contestation? (Fact + light inference from the venue text.)

## Social machine

A live courtroom / mock-trial LARP: roles (judge, counsel, witnesses, the accused object/system) are assigned and played out to interrogate a real technical system. The trial form supplies procedure, evidence, testimony and verdict as a social engine that forces participants to argue accountability into existence. (Fact: "live, role-played court hearing (LARP)"; inference on role mechanics from the trial format and Fornieles' established LARP practice.)

## Fictional device

A counterfactual legal world in which the deodand was never abolished in 1846 but instead survived and was extended to present-day technologies — so that a machine/system can be a defendant. The fiction is a single rule-change (the law persists) rippled forward into the contemporary procurement state. (Fact: the deodand counterfactual; inference that this single legal premise is the generative device.)

## Real-world system it attaches to

English law (the historical deodand; its 1846 abolition under pressure from employers, lawyers and judges as railway accidents rose); UK technology governance via procurement and contracting; the dual-use military/civilian technology economy. (Fact, per venue.)

## Audience / participant role

By invitation only; participants are cast into courtroom roles rather than seated as spectators — they perform the apparatus of justice on the technology. The LARP "will contribute to future public outcomes within the series," so first participants are also co-producers of material for later public programming. (Fact: invitation-only, feeds public outcomes; inference on role-casting.)

## Performer / subject role

The defendant is a thing — a dual-use technology put on trial "as if it were legally responsible for harm." Human players animate prosecution and defence; the real subject under examination is the system of procurement and contracting that renders such technology "common-sense." (Fact + inference.)

## Emotional engine

The drama of the trial — accusation, testimony, the search for someone or something to hold responsible — channeled at an object that cannot answer for itself. The discomfort of trying to locate moral responsibility inside a diffuse sociotechnical/procurement system, and the absurd-but-serious spectacle of indicting a machine. (Inference, grounded in the deodand premise and trial form.)

## Ethical danger

Staging real military/dual-use technology as a defendant risks aestheticising or trivialising actual harm, or letting the human actors and institutions behind procurement decisions hide behind the indicted object — the same displacement of blame the deodand historically performed. Handling a live, politically charged dual-use case demands care about who/what is named. (Inference, flagged for Ed.)

## Aesthetic world

Tags: LARP, law, deodand, dual-use technology, military, governance, procurement, contracts and tenders, accountability, simulation, counterfactual legal history, courtroom. Part of a programme tracing how "algorithmic systems enforce, speculate, and aestheticise power." (Fact + tags.)

## Research method

Collaborative, interdisciplinary research across the n-Space cohort (art + UAL Creative Computing Institute + Goldsmiths Computing + AND + SODA), drawing on legal history (the deodand, its 1846 abolition) and on the mechanics of UK procurement/contracting; developed through the fellowship's workshops and artist-led provocations. (Fact on the institutional/collaborative frame; inference that legal-historical research underpins the script.)

## Use of real material

Real legal history (the deodand and its abolition); a real contemporary dual-use technology as the case; the real UK procurement-and-contracting apparatus as the governing frame. (Fact.)

## Use of fiction

The single counterfactual premise — the deodand still exists and applies to modern technology — plus the fictional courtroom in which an object is a defendant. Fiction is used minimally and procedurally: just enough rule-change to make a real accountability question playable. (Inference.)

## Emergent behaviour

What arguments, verdicts and lines of responsibility emerge when players are forced to prosecute or defend a machine — and whether the trial surfaces accountability that current law cannot. (Inference; outcomes feed "future public outcomes within the series.")

## What this project teaches the studio

That a tightly chosen legal-historical counterfactual can turn an abstract governance critique (procurement, dual-use, accountability) into a playable, embodied trial; and that the studio's LARP/role-play methods scale to collective, cross-disciplinary, institutionally-backed research. (Inference.)

## What failed or remained unresolved

Unknown from public sources — the work is invitation-only and its outcomes were still feeding later public programming as of the 2026-06-10 research date. The named dual-use technology on trial is not disclosed publicly. (Fact: gap.)

## Possible mutations

A public-facing version of the trial; serial trials of different dual-use systems; publication of the "verdicts"; porting the deodand-trial format onto other diffuse systems (algorithms, platforms, financial instruments). (Inference / open.)
