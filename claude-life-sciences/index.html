From One Program to 950 Agents

In 1965, at Stanford, a program called DENDRAL tried to do one thing a chemist does: work out a molecule's structure from its mass spectrum. It is often called the first expert system, the first time a computer was asked to reason about science.

In September 2026, about 950 Claude agents worked through more than 200,000 enzymes in twenty-one hours, narrowing them to twenty proposals for scientists to test. Human researchers in Anthropic's lab took the most promising one to the bench and expressed it. Anthropic reports it as a previously uncharacterized family of array-associated reverse transcriptases. What it does is still unknown, so the lab now has a new question to answer.

Between those two moments lie sixty years of patient work: open databases, blind competitions, two Nobel Prizes, and failures the field learned from. None of it replaced scientists. All of it widened what they could see.

“What AlphaFold achieved is incredible. But what we're talking about here is different.” Jonah Cool, Anthropic

This is the story beneath the headlines about AI: a long, steady, verifiable history, and the exploration it is opening up now.
