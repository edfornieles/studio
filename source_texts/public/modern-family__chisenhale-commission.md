# Modern Family — Ed Fornieles, Modern Family, 2014 (Chisenhale Gallery)
_source: https://chisenhale.org.uk/project/ed-fornieles/ · accessed 2026-06-10_

**Venue:** Chisenhale Gallery, London
**Exhibition:** 18/19 September – 9 November 2014 (preview 18 September, 6:30–8:30pm)
**Photographer (installation views):** Andy Keate

Chisenhale Gallery presented a significant new body of work by artist Ed Fornieles for his inaugural solo UK institutional exhibition. The commission combined a new online work alongside sculpture and performance within an immersive, ambitious installation environment.

The work reimagined the material logic of home improvement, sitcom architecture and the shopping mall, creating a distorted vision of aspirational living — a "Pinterest reality." Visitors encountered an artificial suburban landscape inspired by Pinterest aesthetics and home-décor magazines, blending high-spec finishes and new technology with breakfast cereal and DIY home tiling techniques.

Fornieles employed performer-activated characters derived from television and film, exploring the family unit as a device for the replication and perpetuation of social norms and middle-class American values. The installation questioned the family's role in constructing individual identity within cultural hierarchies, and explored the collapse between online and offline spaces.

## Online commission / data aggregation

A new website forms the basis of the commission. Central to Modern Family is a continually updated aggregating website linked to the APIs (application programming interfaces) of Twitter, Tumblr, Instagram and Google, activated by search terms. The website uses search terms to draw on live content, aggregating news feeds and social-media profiles in real time as stories unfold and people interact online, generating a shifting backdrop to the installation.

To access the website, visitors must sign in with their Facebook login: Fornieles is instigating a trade that begins with you. Your images are then projected into the gallery and viewable to all visitors. The approach mirrors marketing and surveillance methodologies, confronting viewers with the mechanics of digital attention economies, where value is determined by likes, shares and reposts, and drawing on freely available personal and public data.

The exhibition enacts a collapse between the virtual and the real, using the internet as raw material and recapitulating the collected data back into sculpture, installation and performance. Through presenting piles of children's toys covered in mud, or diseased-looking limbs, it casts a critical eye not only on traditional notions of the nurturing and wholesome family home but on the role of advertisers and public bodies in hijacking the data we've offered up for free.
